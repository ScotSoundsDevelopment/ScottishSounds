Scottish sounds market research

A training tool for Speech and Language Therapy (SLT) students on Scottish phonetics

Joshua Keitany

October 2026

**Executive Summary**

Students undertaking speech and language therapy (SLT) course are required to have a level of mastery on phonetic transcription using the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) – a core component in phonetic transcription (clinical phonetics, voice and fluency - University of Manchester). Most SLT courses in the United Kingdom use phonetic transcription tools that rely heavily on Received Pronunciation (RP). This means that practitioners who end up working in Scottish communities will struggle to differentiate between widely accepted regional dialectal variations and the actual speech disorders.

This research analyses the existing phonetic transcription applications and their relevance to the Scottish context. The research samples clinical and educational platforms. The result from the research shows that there is currently no interactive phonetic transcription application that evaluates user input with a sample of Scottish recorded speech and gives feedback. Therefore, there is a clear market need for a phonetic transcription application tailored to the regional dialect variations in Scotland.

1.  **Introduction**
    
1.1. Clinical and educational context

Speech and Language Therapists use phonetic transcription tools to deliver specialist and targeted support to their clients (RCSLT, 2024, RCSLT, 2013). However, the major challenge is being able to determine with certainty whether the client’s condition is because of a speech disorder or a dialect variation. Given that phonetic screening assessments are trained on Received Pronunciation (RP), genuine regional dialect variations in Scotland are flagged as articulation errors. This leads to unnecessary clinical referrals or in the case of education unnecessary additional support.

1.2. Target users

The phonetic transcription application primarily targets the student market. Many universities in the United Kingdom offer Speech and Language Therapy (SLT) courses (University of Strathclyde, University of Manchester, Leeds Beckett University)

Other potential users could be early years teachers and qualified Speech and Language Therapists.

2. **Existing applications**

Some of the existing phonetic transcription tools are:

2.1. Practice IPA [https://www.practise-ipa.com/](https://www.practise-ipa.com/)

2.2. IPA Online – Developed by Newcastle University [https://teaching.ncl.ac.uk/ipa/index.html](https://teaching.ncl.ac.uk/ipa/index.html)

2.3. Seeing Speech – developed by six Scottish Universities. [https://seeingspeech.ac.uk/](https://seeingspeech.ac.uk/)

2.4. Speech STAR – Animation resource developed by Scottish Universities [https://speechstar.ac.uk/](https://speechstar.ac.uk/)

2.5. Dynamic Dialects – developed by six Scottish Universities and it incorporates accents of English from around the world. [https://www.dynamicdialects.ac.uk/](https://www.dynamicdialects.ac.uk/)

2.6. Little Bee Speech – it is a speech therapy software. [https://www.littlebeespeech.com/](https://www.littlebeespeech.com/)

2.7. Microsoft Reading Coach and Google Read Along – these are AI driven

3. **Market needs analysis**

3.1. Practice IPA relies on synthesized audio, and it is not provided in Scottish English.

3.2. IPA Online does not have a user input and feedback mechanism. It also does not present its samples in Scottish English.

3.3. Seeing Speech has some Scottish dialect variations but it lacks the interactivity of user input and feedback.

3.4. Speech STAR provides half-head vocal-tract animations of the sounds of the IPA and extIPA as well English consonants. However, it is a passive resource with no user input and feedback mechanisms.

3.5. Dynamic Dialects just like the previous tools lack the interactivity.

3.6. Little Bee Speech is a speech therapy software that provides interactivity, generates reports and provides feedback to the user. Its focus is speech articulation. However, it lacks the Scottish dialectal variation as it is American English focused.

3.7. Microsoft Reading Coach and Google Read Along provide interactive platforms but they rely on Received Pronunciation. This results in Scottish dialect variations being flagged as articulation errors.

4. **Recommendations**

Scottish English has some unique phonetic features in terms of vowel length, rhoticity, and glottal stop (Ruilan Cao1, a, Shaokang Jin2, SSHR 2017). Therefore, it is important to develop an application that adapts to this uniqueness.

4.1.  The phonetic transcription application should have a customised IPA keyboard to address the Scottish alphabet.

4.2.  The application should give feedback to the user based on phonemic (e.g. user said pan instead of ban) an allophonic (you said wa**t**er instead of wa**ʔ**Ər) transcription.

4.3.  The application could have a feature that allows the user to train on regional accent or clinical speech disorder samples. This choice could be useful for classroom teachers
